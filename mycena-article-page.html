How M&S lost £300m to a credential it didn't control

In April 2025, Marks & Spencer's entire online operation was shut down for weeks. The attacker didn't break in. They logged in — with a credential that belonged to a third party, that M&S had no visibility of, and could not revoke. This is what credential control failure looks like at scale.

On 25 April 2025, Marks & Spencer's website went dark. Over the following weeks, the UK's third-largest retailer watched its online sales — roughly £3.8 billion annually — grind to a halt. The cause was ransomware. The entry point was a credential.

Not a sophisticated zero-day exploit. Not a nation-state attack on critical infrastructure. A credential. One set of login details, held by a third party, that M&S had no way to see, no way to monitor, and no way to revoke — until it was too late.

£300m
Estimated financial impact of the M&S breach, including lost online sales, remediation costs, reputational damage, and share price decline.
(Reuters, May 2025)

What happened

The attack has been attributed to Scattered Spider, a loosely affiliated group known for social engineering and credential-based intrusion rather than technical exploits. According to reporting, the entry point was credentials belonging to a third-party IT provider — one of hundreds of external organisations that hold access to M&S systems as a matter of routine business operation.

The attacker used those credentials to authenticate normally. They didn't trigger anomaly detection because there was no anomaly — from the system's perspective, a legitimate user was logging in. The ransomware was deployed well after initial access, once the attacker had mapped the environment.

"In the physical world, no employer asks an employee to manufacture their own office key. So why do we ask them to do exactly that in the digital world — every day, for every system?"

— Julia O'Toole, Co-CEO, MyCena

Why existing tools failed

M&S, like every major retailer, had invested in cybersecurity. They had endpoint protection, multi-factor authentication for many systems, and a security operations centre. None of it prevented the breach — because none of it addressed the structural problem: the credential was outside M&S's control.

MFA protects a login event. It does not govern who holds the underlying credential, whether that person should still have access, or whether the credential has been shared, sold, or compromised. When the attacker possessed valid credentials and, presumably, a valid MFA token obtained through social engineering, MFA provided no barrier.

PAM tools vault privileged credentials — but the credential in this case was a standard access credential held by a third-party IT provider, not a privileged account in a managed vault. PAM's perimeter did not extend to it.

The credential control gap

Every major breach of the past decade follows the same structural pattern. An attacker obtains a credential. The credential is valid. The credential cannot be revoked quickly enough — either because the organisation doesn't know it's been compromised, or because the credential was held by a third party outside the organisation's governance perimeter.

86%
of web application breaches involve stolen or misused credentials, making credential theft the dominant attack vector for the fifth consecutive year.
(Verizon DBIR 2024)

The solution is not more tools layered on top of the existing credential model. The solution is changing who controls the credential in the first place.

The structural fix

MyCena's Credential Control approach separates identity from access. The organisation — not the user, not the third-party provider — generates, distributes encrypted, and revokes every credential. Users and contractors never see or hold their credentials. They authenticate with one click from their device. The credential is injected at the point of authentication.

In an M&S scenario, the third-party IT provider would have had a credential they could not see, could not share, and could not hand over under social engineering pressure. The moment the relationship changed — or the moment M&S identified suspicious activity — revocation would have taken one command, not days of credential-hunting across dozens of systems.

The implication for financial services

M&S is a retailer, but the attack pattern is identical to what financial services organisations face — and the stakes are higher. Under DORA, financial entities bear direct responsibility for the access governance of their third-party service providers. Under PCI-DSS, every credential touching cardholder data must be governed and auditable. Under the FCA's operational resilience requirements, the ability to recover from a credential compromise within impact tolerances is now a regulatory expectation.

The M&S breach is the clearest recent demonstration that credential control failure is not a theoretical risk. It is a £300 million operational event — and it will happen again, to a financial services firm, with regulatory consequences that dwarf the operational ones.

The question for every CISO and board reading this is not whether credential control is important. It is whether they can demonstrate, right now, who holds every credential that accesses their systems — and how quickly they could revoke all of them.
